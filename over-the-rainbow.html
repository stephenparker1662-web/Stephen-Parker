The “Over the Rainbow” Media Policy

The promised pot of gold just never seems to arrive.

By Stephen Parker · June 2025

Media and Broadcasting Minister Paul Goldsmith has an “over-the-rainbow” quality to the way he runs his portfolio. The promised pot of gold just never seems to arrive. And much has been promised; not so much has materialized.

Admittedly, there have been more signs of life recently. An election drawing close always sparks action. Goldsmith has recently announced that The Broadcasting Standards Authority is to be axed. And then gave an interview saying prominence obligations are going to be rolled out – these are new rules requiring imported televisions to display “prominently” the apps of local broadcasters.

The difficulty here is when and how. With no sign of draft legislation or explanation of how regulations will be shaped – a cynical assessment is these welcome steps announced but reality the can is being kicked down the road.

Goldsmith took over the media portfolio in April 2024. As a senior Minister, he was expected to make a better fist than his predecessor, Melissa Lee, who was sacked by the Prime Minister following a series of missteps.

It is a matter of public record; the media portfolio has proved a poisoned chalice for Ministers past. History is littered with Ministers who start with ambition and end with policy crashes or poor judgements which mar their careers. There is no sign of the trend being bucked.

“With Goldsmith there was some optimism for genuine reform.”

The expectation was built by the Minister himself when he had been handed the portfolio. He seemed to sense the need.

In the wake of troubling industry failures, such as the closure of the Newshub news operation, Goldsmith promised “immediate action” and he signaled steps were needed to “support the media sector,” as he put it.

And Government policy advisers have warned “the sector is facing fundamental challenges to its viability, including sharply declining revenues for local media companies. Significant redundancies, pay cuts, and reductions or closure of services have long-term impacts on sector capability and reduce the range of media choice for New Zealanders.”

By placing a reform package in the public arena, the media sector grew hopeful for change.

The fossilised Broadcasting Act

The first step was to be a rewrite of the fossilised 1989 Broadcasting Act – a law written when newspapers were dominant, and we used landlines to make a call (it would be 19 years before the arrival of the first iPhone 3 in 2008).

A new law could have streamlined splintered funding and regulatory agencies, enacted measures to stop local content being squeezed out by the digital giants, and address how to preserve local media operations.

The government consultation also flagged “prominence” regulations for local broadcasters on smart TVs, boldly placing obligations on streaming platforms such as Netflix and Neon to invest in local content. Or even placing a levy on the big digital platforms to allow reinvestment in the domestic industry.

The sector was also watching what the Coalition Government would do with The Fair Digital News Bargaining Bill, a bill written by Labour and parked in Parliament. Modelled off Australian initiatives, it offered a bargaining system for local news publishers to receive payments from offshore digital giants such as Google and Meta for scraping content. Goldsmith promised to progress the bill after tinkering.

Now 18 months after the industry-wide consultation and a promise of policy boldness, the Beehive has failed to live up to expectation, including the dust-gathering Fair Digital News Bargaining Bill.

Across the ditch

Underscoring the stagnancy of New Zealand’s big ticket media policy is all the action across the ditch.

The Albanese Labor government refuses to bend the knee before the digital giants. It passed a law requiring video on demand streamers – such as Netflix – to spend 7.5 percent of their revenue on Australian content.

Prominence has also been addressed in Australia. Effective from January this year, the government required all newly connected TVs to make it easier to find and navigate to local free-to-air TV services.

For the news publishers, the Australian government is working on an upgrade of its news bargaining laws. This is a serious upgrade of the 2024 news bargaining code. Under the proposed law the digital giants would face a 2.25 percent levy if they do not enter a payment deal with the local news publishers.

Just recently Albanese released a further draft of the News Bargaining Bill, to receive a predictable tough lashing from Google and Meta. Albanese urged the tech giants to reach deals with local media or face the levy. The Australian Government believes up to $250 million will be raised for local journalism.

“Despite the promises, New Zealand has not made it to first base with the Australians taking their second lap.”

Goldsmith had previously indicated he was monitoring Australian steps before pushing New Zealand’s green button.

Coalition constraints

In his defence, his ambitions for the portfolio may have been curtailed by his own coalition partners, and ACT in particular.

In July 2024 David Seymour invoked the “agree to disagree” on the Fair Digital News Bargaining Bill. The ACT argument is media companies need to adapt rather than be supported. It is a fair bet that other media-related reforms would also be vetoed.

And Goldsmith would argue he has lifted the ban on Sunday and public holiday advertising – a step which TVNZ urged – as well as the promise to ban the BSA and introduce prominence regulation (although there is no sight of the enabling legislation).

Does it matter?

Does it matter? Is it important? It depends if you view New Zealand journalism as more than just a commodity. And that a healthy fourth estate is a critical feature of a functioning society.

In Australia, and with many European nations, the view is Google and Meta are too powerful, and the imbalance is killing local journalism. And in the excellent work that emerged from the Ministry of Culture and Heritage consultation document, the case for change is clear.

It leaves a coalition government which is quick to criticise journalism standards – yet reluctant to lift a finger.

Maybe the next Minister will have a crack.
